3. Your "I own all the adapters" idea — yes, and one correction
Your instinct is right and it's the correct strategy. It's essentially the Kamino Scope model, which is proven in production. You don't need Meteora, Raydium, Hylo, or anyone to change a line of code. But one technical correction to how you framed it:

You mostly don't need CPI to read pool state — and mostly can't. Solana programs don't have view functions; there's no "get pool status" instruction to CPI into on Meteora or Raydium. What you do instead is simpler and cheaper: the caller passes the pool's accounts (LbPair, vault token accounts, oracle/observation accounts, etc.) into your adapter instruction, and your adapter deserializes them directly and validates owner, PDA derivation, mint, and program ID. Reading accounts within one transaction is atomic anyway — you get the same-slot consistency guarantee without any CPI, without burning CPI depth (max 4), and it works even on closed-source programs since account layouts are public via IDLs or reverse engineering.

CPI is needed in one specific case: state that must be accrued before it's correct. Lending receipt tokens (Kamino kTokens, marginfi deposits) accrue interest lazily — the stored state is stale until someone calls refresh_reserve/equivalent. There your adapter's refresh tx CPIs their permissionless refresh instruction first, then reads. Still no cooperation needed.

Now the honest trade-offs of owning everything, because they're real:

You become the trusted party. Consumers trust your reimplementation of Meteora's math, not Meteora. That's the whole business — and it's why the moat, such as it is, is audits plus track record plus your methodology specs, not code.

Layout drift is your biggest operational risk. When Meteora upgrades their program or adds a pool type, your adapter can silently misprice. The mitigation is elegant and you should build it into v1: store the target program's last-deployed slot (or programdata hash) in your adapter config, check it on every refresh, and if it changed, set the quote's status flag to STALE until you've reviewed the upgrade. Fail loud, never fail wrong. This single design decision moves the "parser drift" problem from silent to visible.

Spot reserves are not prices. ChatGPT was right to flag this: dividing pool reserves gives a flash-loan-manipulable number. For DEX-traded assets, read the pools' built-in observation/TWAP accounts where they exist (Raydium CLMM observations, Meteora DLMM's oracle account), and tag anything spot-derived as MARKET_SPOT with explicit flags so lenders know never to use it for collateral. Your defensible early ground is protocol-derived values (NAV, exchange rates, redemption) — the ones with deterministic math — not DEX prices, where you'd be competing with Pyth on its home turf.

One simplification your model buys you: you can drop the whole registry / mint-authority / Token-2022 metadata debate from v1. That machinery only matters for the "protocols self-register" version. In your version, you curate the adapter list — exactly like Scope does. A permissionless registry is a v3 problem, if ever.

Where you genuinely can't avoid asking for cooperation: admin-attested values (RWA book NAV) that don't exist on-chain, and — more importantly — the consumer side. You've eliminated the need to ask publishers to change; you still have to convince Pyth, Scope, and lenders to consume. That's a partnerships problem, not a code problem, and it's the actual hard part.

4. Anything else
A few things neither chat told you. Watch compute budgets on the atomic-preview mode: an xSOL NAV computation touching multiple stake pools plus a Pyth update can run tens of thousands of CUs — fine in a dedicated keeper transaction, but consumers may not fit it inside a liquidation tx, so the stored-quote account will be the default read path in practice; design for that. Off-chain cost isn't zero as Gemini claimed, but it's small: a crank bot, redundant RPC, and monitoring, maybe $50–150/month. Think about liability before any lender uses your adapters for real collateral — a mispricing bug that triggers wrong liquidations lands on you reputationally and possibly legally, so audits and explicit disclaimers stop being optional at that point. And strategically, the highest-leverage version of "owning this" might be contributing the valuation interface into the Foundation's sRFC process — being the author of the standard the Foundation adopts beats maintaining a parallel one, and it's the outcome that compounds your reputation most.

Good call — let's make sure the mental model is solid first. I'll build it up piece by piece with one running example, then show you the whole picture.

The one-sentence version
You're building a system where the price of a protocol asset is calculated by a program on-chain, and the result is written to a public account that anyone can read — so nobody ever has to trust an off-chain API doing hidden math.

That's it. Everything else is supporting machinery. Let's use one running example throughout: xSOL, Hylo's leveraged SOL token. Its fair value isn't set by trading — it's defined by a formula: take all the SOL-value locked in Hylo's pools, subtract what's owed to hyUSD holders, divide by how many xSOL exist. All those numbers live in on-chain accounts right now. The problem is purely that nobody has packaged that math into a trustworthy, readable form.

The five pieces
1. The Adapter — the calculator. A program you write, one per protocol. The Hylo adapter knows Hylo's formula and which accounts hold the inputs (Hylo's pool accounts, the Sanctum stake pool accounts, Pyth's SOL/USD feed). Its only job: read those accounts, verify they're the real ones (right owner, right PDA, right mint), do the math, produce a number with a safety range around it.

2. The Core program — the notice board. A tiny, dumb program that owns the official "price notes." It knows nothing about Hylo or Meteora. Its only rules: it accepts a new price note only if a registered adapter signed it (via the adapter's PDA), each new note must have a higher sequence number than the last, and every note gets stamped with when the data was observed and when it expires. Dumb on purpose — dumb is auditable, and one day you can freeze it forever.

3. The Quote account — the price note itself. One PDA per feed (one for xSOL/USD NAV, one for hyUSD backing, etc.). It stores: the value (as "1,000,000,000 base units = 28,431,250 quote units" rather than a decimal price), lower/upper bounds, the slot it was observed at, when it expires, and a hash identifying exactly which formula version produced it. This account is the product. Everything else exists to keep it honest.

4. The Keeper — the guy who rings the bell. Prices don't refresh themselves; some transaction has to trigger the calculation. That's a small bot you run (or anyone runs — it's permissionless) that sends a refresh transaction every N seconds. Costs a fraction of a cent each time.

5. The Consumers — the readers. Pyth, lending protocols, wallets, dashboards. They just read the quote account like any other account.

Walkthrough 1: how a price gets refreshed
Every ~30 seconds, your keeper bot sends one transaction. Here's what happens inside it:

The transaction calls your Hylo adapter's refresh instruction, passing in the accounts it needs: Hylo's pool accounts, the Sanctum stake pool accounts, Pyth's SOL/USD account, and the xSOL quote account.
The adapter doesn't CPI into Hylo — it just deserializes those accounts directly. Solana programs have no view functions; account data is public bytes, so you read them yourself. First thing it does is paranoid verification: is this account really owned by the Hylo program? Is it the right PDA? Right mint? If anything's off, abort. (This is what stops someone passing in a fake pool account stuffed with tokens.)
It computes: total SOL-value of collateral (using stake pool rates × Pyth SOL/USD), minus vUSD liabilities, divided by xSOL supply. All integer math. It also computes a pessimistic and optimistic version using Pyth's confidence interval — that becomes the lower/upper bound.
The adapter CPIs into your core program saying "here's the new xSOL quote, signed by my PDA."
The core checks: is this signer the registered adapter for this feed? Is the sequence number higher than last time? Yes → it overwrites the quote account. Done.
Key subtlety that confused you earlier in the Gemini chat: anyone can send this refresh transaction — it's permissionless, there's no trusted crank operator — but nobody can influence what value gets written, because the value is computed by the adapter code from verified accounts, and only the adapter's PDA can authorize the write. The keeper is just paying for gas. If your bot dies, someone else's bot (or a Hylo bot) can crank it. If nobody cranks it, the quote goes stale and its expiry slot makes that visible — it fails loud, never lies.

Walkthrough 2: how a price gets used
Two modes, for two kinds of customers:

Mode A — read the note (99% of usage). Kamino wants to value xSOL collateral. Their program takes the quote account as an input, reads it (one account read, a few hundred CUs), checks "is valid_until_slot still in the future? Is the status flag healthy?" and uses the lower bound for collateral. Pyth's mirror API does the same thing off-chain: fetch the account through two RPCs, check they match byte-for-byte, decode, serve as JSON. The API is a photocopier — it can't lie about the price because anyone can compare its output against the account.

Mode B — call the calculator live (high-stakes only). A liquidator worried the 30-second-old quote is too stale can include your adapter's compute in their own transaction — the price gets computed from this exact slot's state, atomically, in the same transaction as the liquidation. More accounts, more compute, but zero staleness. Most integrators never need this.

The mental model in one picture
Think of it like a company's accounting:

Hylo's raw accounts are the receipts — all the truth is in there, but scattered and unreadable.
Your adapter is the accountant — a robot accountant whose procedure is published (the methodology hash) and who refuses to work with unverified receipts.
The quote account is the audited financial statement posted on the wall — one page, standard format, dated, with a "valid until" stamp.
The core program is the rule that only the accountant may post statements to the wall, and each new statement must be newer than the last.
Pyth's mirror API is a photocopy of the statement — useful for people who won't come to the office, and verifiable against the original by anyone.
The observer is an independent second accountant who re-does the math from the same receipts every few minutes; if his number ever differs from the wall, alarms go off and photocopying stops.
The whole design reduces to one principle: there is no point in the pipeline where a human or a hackable server chooses the number. The number is a pure function of on-chain state, computed by public code, and everything downstream only transports it.

What "adding a new protocol" looks like
This is where your "I own all adapters" plan slots in. Supporting Meteora DAMM v2 LP tokens means: write one new adapter program that knows DAMM v2's account layout (which you already know from your 2025 work), register its PDA in the core as the authority for those feeds, and point a keeper at it. Meteora never hears from you. Same for Sanctum LSTs, Kamino kTokens, anything. The core, quote format, mirror API, and observer are all shared — each new protocol is only a new calculator plugged into existing rails.

Does the picture click now? If yes, the natural next question to pressure-test your understanding is: for the xSOL feed specifically, which accounts does the adapter need and what's the exact formula — that's the methodology spec, and writing it is where planning actually starts.

Good questions — all three are the kind of detail that separates a working oracle from an exploitable one, so worth getting fully clear.

Q1: Staleness/accrual — "is the number in the account the real number?"
On-chain accounts don't update themselves. A value stored in an account is only as fresh as the last transaction that wrote it. But some values change continuously in reality even when no transaction runs.

Bank analogy: your account earns interest daily, but the bank only credits it on the 1st of the month. On the 20th, your statement says $1,000, but you've really "earned" $1,004 — four dollars exist in reality that aren't written down yet. If someone values your account, should they say $1,000 (what's stored) or $1,004 (what's true)?

Hylo has exactly this pattern in two places, per their own docs:

LST yield is "harvested per epoch." hyloSOL quietly appreciates as staking rewards accrue, but Hylo's TVL accounting may only reflect that when a harvest transaction runs.
The BTC borrow rate: the pool owes, say, 0.01% of the vault per epoch as a fee. If the harvest hasn't run for two epochs, the vault account still shows the full balance — but the true TVL is slightly lower, because those fees are owed but not yet deducted.
So when your adapter reads Hylo's accounts and computes xSOL NAV, the question for Plish is: is my result the true current NAV, or the NAV as of the last crank? If there's drift, you have three options: (a) your refresh transaction CPIs Hylo's harvest/update instruction first, then reads — this is exactly how lending integrations call refresh_reserve before reading a reserve; (b) your adapter applies the accrual formula itself (needs the formula and config from them); or (c) accept the lag and document it in the methodology — fine if the drift is a few basis points, dangerous if it's not. You can't choose between a/b/c without asking, which is why it's Plish question #1.

Q3: Rounding direction — "when you must lose a decimal, which way do you fall?"
On-chain math is integer-only — no floats, no decimals. So division must throw something away. 100 ÷ 3 is not 33.333; it's either 33 (round down) or 34 (round up). The rule for which way to round is the "rounding direction," and in DeFi it's a security property, not a formatting detail.

Concrete example: suppose Hylo's SOL pool has TVL − vUSD = 1,000,000,007 lamport-units of value, and xSOL supply is 3. True NAV per xSOL = 333,333,335.67. Your quote must say either 333,333,335 or 333,333,336. Which?

The principle: always round against the person who benefits from the number. A lender using your quote to value collateral should get the rounded-down figure — you must never tell a lender the collateral is worth more than it is. If you were valuing someone's debt, you'd round up — never understate what's owed. One unit sounds laughably small, but rounding in the wrong direction is a classic exploit family: attackers loop an operation thousands of times, pocketing the fraction each time, and drain vaults with it.

For your spec this means two things. First, your quote fields have explicit rules: quote_amount and lower_quote_amount round down, upper_quote_amount rounds up — conservative in both directions. Second, your adapter's rounding must match Hylo's own (which is why reading hylo-core or asking Plish matters): if Hylo floors where you ceil, your NAV and their redemption math disagree by one unit, and your independent observer will constantly fire false divergence alarms over dust.

Q4: Why care what Pyth asked for?
Because Pyth is customer #1, and the customer has already written your product requirements — they're just sitting in Plish's DMs instead of yours.

The whole strategic play of the mirror API is: Pyth said "give us an API," and instead of arguing that APIs are unsafe, you hand them exactly the API they asked for — same feeds, same JSON shape, same update cadence — except yours does zero math and is byte-verifiable against the chain. From Pyth's side, adopting it costs nothing; it's the thing they requested. That's what makes adoption plausible.

But that only works if you match the ask. If Pyth asked Hylo for xSOL/USD and hyUSD/USD updated every 10 seconds in a specific publisher format, and you show up with an eHYUSD exchange rate updated every 5 minutes in your own creative schema, they'll shrug and wait for Hylo's own API — and your single best adoption story dies on a formatting mismatch. Knowing the ask tells you three concrete things: which feeds to build descriptors for first, what cadence your keeper must sustain (which sets your infra design and cost), and what response format the mirror must emit so Pyth's side needs zero integration work.

Restaurant version: a customer already placed an order. You can cook it brilliantly, but first you need to read the ticket.
